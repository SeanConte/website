I investigate how a community’s structure shapes its epistemic capacities.

I use formal models to study how features of a community, such as consensus, disagreement, reliability, and resilience, emerge from how its members interact and from the structures those interactions build. I look at how seemingly irrational behavior by individuals can combine to make the group as a whole rational, and how individuals who increase their own expected accuracy can decrease their community’s by doing so. I apply these results to scientific communities and to public discourse shaped by social media and AI.

The projects fit into a larger picture of feedback between a community and the institutions through which it inquires. Journals, review norms, platforms, and AI systems shape the options open to members. The choices members make produce collective dynamics, and those dynamics reshape the institutions in turn.

I treat AI as an institution of this kind. A system that many people consult belongs to the structure a community inquires through, alongside a shared dataset or a review process, so I model it there instead of adding it to the network as another agent.

Mereological conflict

Paper in preparation

The modal and social variance result and the wise cascades result are both instances of a class of problems I call mereological conflicts, in which every member of a community can raise their own expected accuracy by doing what lowers the community’s. Suppose each of seven members can either rely on a source with accuracy 0.7 whose errors are independent of everyone else’s, or share a single source with accuracy 0.75, such as a common dataset or an AI system that many people consult. Each member does better with the shared source. The probability that a majority is correct falls from 0.87 to 0.75, because shared evidence means shared failures.

These conflicts differ from familiar collective action problems. In a tragedy of the commons, individuals gain at one another’s expense. Here no member gains at another’s expense, and the community is nothing over and above its members, yet each member can expect to do better while the community expects to do worse. The conflict lives in modal space, in what each member and the community can expect across possible outcomes, rather than in any single run. Because it requires neither irrational nor self-interested members, it can explain collective epistemic failures that would otherwise be attributed to one or the other.

Wise cascades

Complex Networks 2026, Granada, December 2026Full paper in preparation

Information cascades, in which people set aside their own evidence and defer to those before them, are usually treated as a failure of rationality, and the wisdom of crowds as a success. I add a single parameter τ to the canonical cascade model, governing how much weight agents give to their peers’ votes. At τ = 0 the model is the wisdom of crowds model, and at τ = 1 it is the cascade model. The parameter can be read as a community’s trust in its members’ judgments, or as its evidential standard for when to stop inquiring and defer.

At intermediate values a community still enters a cascade, though only after a longer run of independent votes, and the cascade it enters is far more likely to be right. The wise cascades theorem says that for any desired level of reliability, some τ and population size guarantee a cascade correct to that level. A community can acquire reliable authority that none of its members has, and past a certain point its members can ignore their own evidence without epistemic detriment.

The extension I am presenting at Complex Networks runs many parallel lanes of agents that sometimes communicate, so that cascades can form locally while remaining defeasible globally.

The conservation of evidential variance

Application to populations of LLM agents at a NeurIPS 2026 workshop

The modal and social trade-off is one case of a more general decomposition. The variance of the evidence a community gathers divides into three parts. Individual variance is the spread among the samples within a single member’s evidence. Social variance is the spread between members. Modal variance is the spread between the community’s possible aggregate outcomes.

EV = IV + SV + MV

The total is fixed by the random variable being studied. How much each member tests, and whose results each member pools with their own, decide where the variance sits. The identity holds however the members go on to process their evidence.

Modal and social variance

Presented at the Formal Epistemology Workshop, 2026Under review

Evidence produced by a probabilistic process varies from sample to sample, and judgments formed from that evidence inherit the variation. The variance divides into social variance, the disagreement among a community’s members, and modal variance, the unpredictability of where the community as a whole ends up. The split follows from the law of total variance, and the trade-off between modal and social variance depends on the degree of overlap between the members’ evidence. Greater overlap makes members more alike and makes the community’s outcome less predictable.

This gives one account of why diversity in evidence is valuable to a community.

Selective sharing

Presented at the Formal Epistemology Workshop, 2020Developed in my dissertation

A community can polarize without anyone lying. If agents share only the evidence they believe will improve their peers’ accuracy, the community can form echo chambers that end in entrenchment or group-think, and these echo chambers form even when no one is cut off from opposing views.

Every result a selective agent reports is real. The record they leave behind can still mislead as badly as fabricated data, which gives a way to model bias without any dishonest agent. I plan to return to this work with the variance framework, asking how sharing strategies interact with the structure of a community’s evidence.

  • Network trust and disclosure model

Twelve labs sit on a ring and test a new treatment B against a standard treatment that works half the time. B actually works 45% of the time, so the right conclusion is that B is worse. Each round, every lab runs ten trials and updates on its own results and, at half weight, on what its neighbors report. A selective lab reports a batch only when it currently believes B is better and the batch agrees. Selective labs are spread evenly around the ring.

This is the trade between social and modal variance from above, pushed to extremes. With sparse connections, labs split into camps that entrench on opposite answers, which is social variance. Raise the connectivity and group-think becomes more likely; when the community fails together in some runs and not others, that is modal variance. Selective sharing has little effect until most labs do it.

Equivalent fabrication

Human–AI inquiry and the topology of epistemic dependence

Next project, in development

This project extends the fabric of the cascade model to communities that rely on AI. No single AI system observes the whole community. Different parts of an information infrastructure adjust to local behavior: frequently consulted sources become easier to find, neglected sources become harder to discover, and some questions become easier to pursue than others.

I model this as an adaptive network in which agents keep their private evidence, their reliability, and their willingness to defer, while the systems that recommend sources to them update according to how those recommendations are used. The question is how the resulting dynamics shape a community’s resilience, its capacity to withstand misleading evidence, and its plasticity, its capacity to revise its beliefs when new evidence arrives.

Here the institution is the part of the loop that adapts. The recommending systems change the options open to members, the members’ use of those options produces collective dynamics, and the dynamics feed back into what the systems recommend next.

The variance result says what to look for. As a community’s informational routes narrow, variance shifts from disagreement among members to unpredictability in the community’s outcome, so a community can grow more uniform while becoming more likely to fail together.

Dissertation

From Evidence to Opinions: Exploring Variance in Scientific Communities

Ph.D., Texas A&M University, 2023

Much of this work began as questions in my dissertation, which studied networks of scientists who each produce their own statistical research and share it with those they are connected to. Its models vary network structure along with strategies for sharing, trusting, and updating, and its explanations of polarization and group-think rest on the nature of statistical data and how it is distributed, whether or not the agents themselves are rational.
